A shadow map sees the scene from the light, in texels: far away or for thin
shapes, its shadows blur, float or leak. A **ray** is exact: from the point
being lit, towards the light; if it hits something on the way, the point is
in shadow.

## The traced scene

Rays need the whole scene at once, in **acceleration structures**: a
bounding volume hierarchy per mesh, and one over their instances.
`RayScene` builds and keeps them:

```ts
import { RayScene } from '@fdw/engine-raytracing';

// Where the backend traces rays: Vulkan (in hardware), WebGPU (in
// software, a BVH traversed in WGSL). Not WebGL2.
const scene = backend.capabilities?.rayQuery
  ? new RayScene(backend)
  : undefined;

// Each frame: the items (or a source: a scene graph) as they are now.
scene?.update({ items });
```

The structure of a mesh is built once, at its first update; the instances
are rewritten each time: an item moved moves its shadow at the next frame.

## Tracing the shadows

```ts
renderer.render({
  items,
  camera,
  surface,
  lights: [sun],
  rays: { scene, shadows: true },
});
```

The renderer compiles the materials with shadows by rays: in the loop over
the lights of their shading model, `light.shadow` traces a ray towards each
light that casts shadows (`castShadow`), from the point moved a little
along its normal (`offset`, 2 cm by default). No shadow map is drawn.

Directional and spot lights cast shadows, as with the shadow maps. The
transparent items and those with `castShadow: false` are not in the way
of the shadow rays.

## Soft shadows

A real light has a size: the sun is half a degree wide, and the edge of a
shadow fades over a distance that grows with the distance to what casts it.
Give the light its size, and several rays per pixel:

```ts
// A sun of half a degree: the tangent of its angular radius. A spot: its
// radius, in meters.
sun.shadow = { softness: 0.03 };
renderer.render({ …, rays: { scene, shadows: { samples: 8 } } });
```

The rays leave on a spiral across the disk of the light, turned from pixel
to pixel: with 8 rays, the penumbra is smooth up close and a little grainy
in large ones. Each ray costs: the samples multiply the cost of the
shadows.

## Leaves and holes

A masked material (`alphaMode: 'mask'`) cuts its pixels below its alpha
cutoff: leaves, fences, the perforated screen of the example. The rays go
through the cut parts too: its shadow has the holes.

`RayScene` bakes, once per masked material (again when its values change),
its **alpha mask**: its surface evaluated over its uv, 256 × 256 bits, kept
in the buffers of the scene. The rays of the shadows, of the occlusion and
of the path tracer test the triangles of the masked instances against it
during the traversal (on Vulkan, the candidate hits of the ray query; in
WebGPU, the triangles of the BVH), at the uv of the point: no square
shadows of the leaves. What the alpha reads of the uv (its textures) is in
the mask; what it reads of the position or of the object is not.

## On each backend

On **Vulkan**, the GPU traverses its own structures (`VK_KHR_ray_query`):
traced shadows cost little more than shadow maps. On **WebGPU**, there is
no ray tracing API: the backend builds the BVH on the CPU, and the programs
traverse it in WGSL, a stack of nodes per ray — fine for scenes of a few
hundred thousand triangles, slower beyond. **WebGL2** has neither: keep the
shadow maps there, as the example does.
