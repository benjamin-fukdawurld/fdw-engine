A [field of heights](/fdw-engine/tutorials/shallow-water/) cannot splash:
it has one height of water per column. For water that falls, breaks and
splashes, `ParticleFluid` simulates **thousands of particles** on the GPU,
each a small volume of water that keeps its distance from its neighbors.

## Particles

```ts
import { ParticleFluid } from '@fdw/engine-fluids';

const liquid = new ParticleFluid(backend, {
  count: 16384, // a power of two: the most it holds
  radius: 0.02, // m: at rest, the particles are 2 radii apart
  bounds: { min: [-1.2, 0, -0.5], max: [1.2, 1.5, 0.5] },
});
liquid.addBlock([-1.2, 0, -0.5], [-0.3, 0.9, 0.5]); // 12 100 particles

// Each frame:
liquid.update(dt);
```

The box `bounds` holds the particles (its walls). `add(positions,
velocity)` adds particles anywhere (a stream poured in), `clear()` removes
them all.

| Option       | Default | What it does                                        |
| ------------ | ------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| `count`      | 16384   | The particles it can hold (a power of two)          |
| `radius`     | 0.05    | The radius of a particle, in meters                 |
| `bounds`     | —       | The box of the particles                            |
| `substeps`   | 2       | The steps of a frame                                |
| `iterations` | 4       | The iterations of the constraints of a step         |
| `viscosity`  | 0.02    | How much each particle follows its neighbors (XSPH) |
| `tensile`    | 0.03    | The artificial pressure against clumping            |
| `maxSpeed`   | 8       | The fastest a particle goes, in m/s                 |

## A step

The solver is **position-based** (Macklin and Müller, _Position Based
Fluids_): instead of pressures and forces, which need tiny steps to stay
stable, it moves the particles until the density is the same everywhere.

1. **The prediction**: each particle falls for dt, from its velocity.
2. **The sort**: the key of each particle is its cell (cells of 4 radii,
   the reach of a particle); a **bitonic sort** on the GPU orders the
   particles by key. Then the start and the end of each cell in the sorted
   list: the neighbors of a particle are those of the 27 cells around it,
   contiguous in memory. A bitonic sort needs no atomics (which the
   compute graphs do not have, nor WebGL2): each of its stages is a
   dispatch where each element keeps the smaller or the larger of itself
   and its partner. For 16 384 particles: 105 dispatches.
3. **The constraints** (4 iterations): the density of each particle (the
   kernel summed over its neighbors), how far it is from the density at
   rest, and how the particle and its neighbors must move to cancel it;
   then the moves. A little artificial pressure (`tensile`) keeps the
   particles from clumping at the surface. The walls and the bodies push
   the particles out.
4. **The velocities**: from the motion of the step, smoothed by a
   viscosity (each particle drawn toward the mean of its neighbors).

The CPU runs the same step (`stepParticles`), in the same order: the
rendering tests check that both agree on every backend.

**Stability.** A splash against a wall packs particles between the wall
and the water that comes. Too few iterations, or too much artificial
pressure, and they blow apart: 4 iterations and a `tensile` of 0.03 keep a
dam that breaks in a tank of 2.4 m calm, at 2 steps of 1/120 s a frame.

## Drawing a liquid

```ts
import { ParticleFluidRenderer } from '@fdw/engine-fluids';

const liquidRenderer = new ParticleFluidRenderer(renderer, {
  environment: sky,
}).setSun(light);

// Each frame, after liquid.update(dt):
liquidRenderer.render({ liquid, items, lights: [light], camera, surface });
```

Particles drawn as spheres look like beads. The renderer draws a surface
**in screen space** (van der Laan, Green and Sainz):

1. **The depth of the liquid**: each particle a square turned to the
   camera, its pixels out of the circle discarded, the depth of the front
   of its sphere written by pixel; tested against the depth of the scene;
2. **The smoothing**: a bilateral filter of that depth, across then down,
   three times; it averages the neighbors at a similar depth over about a
   particle on the screen: the spheres melt into a surface, the edges stay;
3. **The light**: the normals from the depths around, then the water of the
   sea: the sky reflected by Fresnel, the sun, the scene behind bent and
   absorbed along the thickness of liquid (the depth of the scene, minus
   the depth of the liquid).

The look is that of the water of the sea (`WaterLook`): the example tints
it blue-green with a stronger `absorption`.

## Bodies in the liquid

<Example name="splash" />

Drag to turn around, the wheel to zoom; **Space** drops a crate, **P**
pours water in, **R** breaks the dam again.

```ts
const bodies = new FluidBodies(backend);
bodies.add(crate);
const buoyancy = new Buoyancy(liquid, { density: 1000 });
buoyancy.add(crate, { points: boxPoints(half), volume });

// Each frame:
bodies.update();
liquid.update(dt, bodies);
```

**The bodies push the particles**: in each step, a particle inside a body
(a sphere or a box of `FluidBodies`) is moved out of it; its velocity
follows from the move. The paddle of the first example sweeps the water
that way.

**The liquid carries the bodies.** After each update, a dispatch per column
of cells finds the surface of the liquid above it (from the top, the first
cell with a few particles, and the highest of them) and its velocity; read
back a frame late, they are `heightAt` and `velocityAt` for the `Buoyancy`
of the ocean: the crates float, carried by the flow.

## In WebGL2

WebGL2 has no compute shaders: the compute graphs run as draws into
textures ([the compute guide](/fdw-engine/guides/compute/)). The 120
dispatches of a step of particles cost much more there: the examples use
4 096 particles of 3 cm in WebGL2, 16 384 of 2 cm in WebGPU.
