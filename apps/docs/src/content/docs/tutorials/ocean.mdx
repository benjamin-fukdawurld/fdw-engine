`@fdw/engine-ocean` draws an open sea. Its waves come from one of two
sources:

- **An ocean spectrum** (Tessendorf): thousands of waves of every length and
  direction, of amplitudes given by an oceanographic spectrum (JONSWAP or
  Phillips), summed by inverse FFTs on the GPU at each frame;
- **Gerstner waves**: a few trains of parallel crests, summed in the vertex
  shader.

The same `Ocean` draws both, and gives the height of the water to the CPU
(see [Floating objects](/fdw-engine/tutorials/floating-objects/)).

## The waves of a spectrum

```ts
import { OceanSpectrum } from '@fdw/engine-ocean';

const spectrum = new OceanSpectrum(backend, {
  windSpeed: 10, // m/s, 10 m above the sea
  windDirection: Math.PI / 2, // blowing toward +z
});
```

The options describe the sea, not the waves:

| Option           | Default            | What it does                                                                       |
| ---------------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `model`          | `'jonswap'`        | `jonswap`: a sea built by the wind over a fetch; `phillips`: a fully developed sea |
| `windSpeed`      | 8                  | The speed of the wind, in m/s: the higher, the longer and higher the waves         |
| `windDirection`  | 0                  | Where the wind blows to, in radians from +x toward +z                              |
| `fetch`          | 100 km             | JONSWAP: how far the wind has blown over the sea                                   |
| `spread`         | 8                  | How much the crests keep to the direction of the wind                              |
| `choppiness`     | 1                  | The horizontal motion of the water: sharper crests, wider troughs                  |
| `cascades`       | `[233, 37.7, 6.3]` | The sides of the tiles, in meters                                                  |
| `resolution`     | 128                | The points along a side of a tile (a power of 2)                                   |
| `period`, `seed` | 200 s, 1           | The animation loops over the period; the seed gives another sea                    |

On the CPU, once: each wave vector **k** of a tile gets a random complex
amplitude, of variance given by the spectrum. On the GPU, at each frame
(`update(time)`): the amplitudes are turned by e^(iωt), ω = √(g|k|) (deep
water waves: the long ones run faster), then **eight fields** come out of
inverse FFTs: the height, the horizontal motion (x and z), and their
derivatives. They are packed two by two in complex numbers (a + ib), four
complex FFTs instead of eight.

**The cascades.** One tile repeats every 233 m, which shows from above.
Three tiles of sizes that do not divide one another each keep a band of
wave lengths: the first one the swell, the last one the ripples. Their sum
does not repeat.

**On every backend.** The FFTs are compute graphs: a spectrum, the 2 × 7
stages of butterflies along the rows then the columns, a last pass for the
fields. WebGPU and Vulkan run them as compute shaders; WebGL2 runs the
same graphs by rendering to textures ([Compute](/fdw-engine/guides/compute/#on-webgl2)),
without a change to the code. The fields stay on the GPU, in two storage
buffers read by the material of the sea.

## Gerstner waves

```ts
import { GerstnerWaves } from '@fdw/engine-ocean';

const gerstner = new GerstnerWaves([
  { direction: 1.5, wavelength: 40, amplitude: 0.6, steepness: 0.7 },
  { direction: 1.9, wavelength: 23, amplitude: 0.32, steepness: 0.6 },
  // …
]);
```

Each point of the surface turns on a circle: the crests sharpen, the
troughs widen. `steepness`, from 0 (a sine) to 1, sets how much. A few
waves, computed in the vertex shader from their constants and their
phases (uniforms updated each frame, exact at any time): no compute pass,
no FFT, and an exact height on the CPU. Good for a calm sea, a lake, a
stylized look; the spectrum looks like the open sea.

## The ocean

```ts
import { createSky, Ocean } from '@fdw/engine-ocean';

const sky = createSky(backend, { sun });
const ocean = new Ocean({ waves: spectrum, environment: sky, level: 0 });
ocean.setSun(light); // the direction and color of a DirectionalLight
```

`Ocean` is a render item: a **grid of rings around the camera**, each with
cells twice as large as the one inside it, then a flat skirt to the
horizon (20 km at least). `update(cameraPosition, time)` moves it under the camera by
steps of its coarsest cell: its vertices stay on the same points of the
world, and the waves do not swim under them. Where two rings meet, the
vertices of the finer one fall on those of the coarser one: no cracks.

The material moves the vertices by the waves, each fading out with the
distance before the vertices get too far apart for it (`fade`, 2 lengths
by default). The pixels then read the derivatives of the waves: the
normal, and the **jacobian** of the motion, below 1 where the crests
squeeze the water, where the foam forms. The waves too small for a pixel
fade out, and make the water rougher by the variance of their slopes:
the far sea turns into a soft sheen instead of shimmering.

## The light of the water

- **The reflection** of the environment (a cube texture: the sky of
  `createSky`, or yours), weighted by the Fresnel term of water: 2 %
  looking down, all of it at grazing angles. The rougher the water, the
  blurrier the reflection (a higher level of the texture).
- **The sun**: a GGX highlight, the glitter of the waves.
- **What is seen through the water**: the scene behind it, bent by the
  waves, absorbed along the depth of water (the red first), and the light
  scattered by the water itself (`scatterColor`).
- **The sun through the crests**: lighter, greener crests toward the sun.
- **The foam**, where the jacobian falls below `foamThreshold`, and along
  the shores, broken by a noise.

`ocean.setLook({ … })` changes them at any time: `scatterColor`,
`absorption`, `subsurfaceColor`, `foamColor`, `roughness`, `foamThreshold`,
`foamAmount`, `shoreFoam`, `refraction`, `environmentIntensity`.

## Drawing it

```ts
const renderer = new Renderer(backend);
const oceanRenderer = new OceanRenderer(renderer, { toneMapping: 'neutral' });

onFrame((time) => {
  ocean.update(camera.position, time);
  oceanRenderer.render({ ocean, source: scene, camera, surface, time });
});
```

To see what is under the water, the sea is drawn after the rest:

1. the scene (`source`, `items`), with its view depth;
2. the sky where nothing was drawn, copied for the water;
3. the sea, tested against the depth of the scene, reading the copy
   and the depth: the depth of water at each pixel;
4. the tone mapping, to the surface.

`addPasses(graph, options)` adds the first three to a render graph of
yours and returns the image, in linear colors. Without what is behind
(`refraction: false` on the `Ocean`), the sea is opaque and draws as any
item, with `renderer.render`.
