A parametric surface is a function: two numbers `(u, v)` in, a point of
space out. Sampled on a grid of `(u, v)`, it gives the vertices of a
mesh; the grid gives its triangles. Nothing in the engine is specific to
it: a `Mesh` is built from arrays, and the arrays can come from a formula.

In the first example, two surfaces are made of **two curves drawn in
orthogonal planes**, in red and in blue. The curves change with time, and
the surfaces follow.

<Example name="parametric-surfaces" />

## A surface, a grid

The surface is sampled on `(columns + 1) × (rows + 1)` points. The normal
is the cross product of the two partial derivatives `∂S/∂u` and `∂S/∂v`,
computed here by central differences; with a formula, they can be
derived by hand too. The uv are `(u, v)` themselves:

```ts
type ParametricSurface = (u: number, v: number) => Vec3;

for (let j = 0; j <= rows; j++) {
  for (let i = 0; i <= columns; i++) {
    const u = i / columns;
    const v = j / rows;
    const du = sub(S(Math.min(u + h, 1), v), S(Math.max(u - h, 0), v));
    const dv = sub(S(u, Math.min(v + h, 1)), S(u, Math.max(v - h, 0)));
    const n = normalize(cross(du, dv));
    data.set([...S(u, v), ...n, u, v], (j * (columns + 1) + i) * 8);
  }
}
```

Each cell of the grid makes two triangles, counterclockwise around
`du × dv`: the front side is the one the normal points to. An open
surface is seen from both sides; the example writes every vertex twice,
the second time with its normal reversed, and the triangles of the back
side clockwise. (`doubleSided` in the render state of the material draws
the back faces, but with the normal of the front.)

The lines drawn on the surfaces are the **iso-curves**: `u` constant, or
`v` constant, every sixteenth. The material reads them from the uv, a
pixel wide at any distance with `fwidth`.

## Two curves, two orthogonal planes

### The translation surface

On the left, the curve **A** is drawn in the plane `z = 1.5`, the curve
**B** in the plane `x = -1.5`, orthogonal to the first. They start from
the same corner `O = A(0) = B(0)`. The surface moves one curve along the
other:

```ts
const translation: ParametricSurface = (u, v) => sub(add(A(u), B(v)), O);
```

For `v = 0`, it is `A`; for `u = 0`, it is `B`: the two curves are two
edges of the surface. Every iso-curve `v` constant is a copy of `A`,
moved by `B(v) - O`; every iso-curve `u` constant is a copy of `B`.
When the wave of `A` is flat, the surface is a cylinder of `B`: the arch
moved along a straight line.

Since the curves are in orthogonal planes, the height of a point is the
sum of the two heights: `y = a(x) + b(z)`. Draw a profile across, a
profile along, and every point of the surface is defined.

### The spherical product

On the right, the **meridian** (in red) is drawn in the plane XY: a
radius `r(u)` and a height `y(u)`. The **section** (in blue) is drawn in
the plane XZ: a closed curve `(x(v), z(v))`. The surface scales the
section by the radius at each height:

```ts
const product: ParametricSurface = (u, v) => {
  const [x, z] = section(v);
  return [radius(u) * x, height(u), radius(u) * z];
};
```

With a circle for the section, it is the surface of revolution of the
meridian: a lathe. The section of the example is a **superellipse**,
`|x|ⁿ + |z|ⁿ = 1`, a circle for `n = 2`, closer and closer to a square as
`n` grows:

```ts
const section = (v: number): [number, number] => {
  const c = Math.cos(2 * Math.PI * v);
  const s = Math.sin(2 * Math.PI * v);
  return [
    Math.sign(c) * Math.abs(c) ** (2 / n),
    Math.sign(s) * Math.abs(s) ** (2 / n),
  ];
};
```

The radius of the meridian is 1 at the bottom: there, the surface is the
section itself; and for `v = 0`, the section is the point `(1, 0)`: the
surface is the meridian. Here again, the two curves lie on the surface.

## When a curve changes

The surfaces are sampled again on every frame, with the new curves, and
the mesh takes the new vertices; the indices do not change:

```ts
onFrame((time) => {
  amplitude = 0.45 * Math.cos(time * 0.5);
  n = 2 + 3 * (0.5 - 0.5 * Math.cos(time * 0.7));
  mesh.setVertices(0, sample(product, columns, rows));
});
```

`setVertices` uploads the whole buffer again; `updateVertices` writes
only a range of it, for a surface changing in one place. For a surface of
millions of points changing on every frame, the sampling belongs on the
GPU: see the [tessellation](/fdw-engine/tutorials/tessellation/), whose
vertices are computed in the vertex shader.

## Pipes

A pipe sweeps a **circle along a curve of space**. The curve is a list of
points; on each point, a circle is set in the plane normal to the curve;
consecutive circles are joined by quads, as the rows of the grid above.

<Example name="pipes" />

### A frame on each point

On the point `p` of tangent `t`, the circle needs two directions `r` and
`s` normal to `t` and to each other:

```ts
for (let k = 0; k <= sides; k++) {
  const angle = (2 * Math.PI * k) / sides;
  const normal = add(scale(r, Math.cos(angle)), scale(s, Math.sin(angle)));
  vertices.push(...add(p, scale(normal, radius)), ...normal, length, k / sides);
}
```

The normal of the vertex is the direction from the axis; the uv are the
length along the curve and the turn around it. The vertex `k = sides` is
on the vertex `k = 0`, with a `v` of 1: the texture closes the circle.

For a curve in a plane, the frame is easy: `s` is the normal of the
plane, `r = s × t` — the curves of the first example are drawn so. In
space, the classic frame of a curve is the **Frenet frame**: `r` towards
the center of curvature. It is not a good choice for a pipe:

- on a straight part, there is no curvature: `r` is not defined;
- at an inflection, the center of curvature goes to the other side: `r`
  turns over in a single step;
- the frame turns around the tangent as fast as the curve twists: the
  pipe looks wrung.

### Rotation-minimizing frames

The frames used by the pipes turn as little as possible around the
tangent from one point to the next. The **double reflection** of Wang,
Jüttler, Zheng and Liu (2008) computes them in a few lines: the frame of
a point is reflected in the plane bisecting the chord to the next point,
which sends `p` on the next point; a second reflection, in the plane
between the reflected tangent and the next tangent, aligns the tangents.

```ts
const v1 = sub(point, previous.point);
const c1 = dot(v1, v1);
const rL = sub(previous.r, scale(v1, (2 / c1) * dot(v1, previous.r)));
const tL = sub(previous.t, scale(v1, (2 / c1) * dot(v1, previous.t)));
const v2 = sub(t, tL);
const c2 = dot(v2, v2);
r = c2 < 1e-12 ? rL : sub(rL, scale(v2, (2 / c2) * dot(v2, rL)));
```

The first frame takes any `r` normal to the tangent. Two reflections make
a rotation: the frame stays a frame, and on a straight part, it does not
move. The stripes of the pipes show it: they run along the curve without
spiraling.

### Closed curves

On a closed curve, the frame propagated once around comes back on the
first point **turned** by an angle, the holonomy of the curve: the
stripes would not meet. The example adds one frame on the first point,
measures the angle between it and the first frame, and turns each frame
back by its share of the length:

```ts
const twist = Math.atan2(
  dot(cross(last.r, first.r), first.t),
  dot(last.r, first.r),
);
for (const frame of result) {
  const angle = (twist * frame.length) / last.length;
  frame.r = add(
    scale(frame.r, Math.cos(angle)),
    scale(frame.s, Math.sin(angle)),
  );
  frame.s = cross(frame.t, frame.r);
}
```

The knot of the example is a **trefoil**, `(sin a + 2 sin 2a,
cos a − 2 cos 2a, −sin 3a)`: the stripes meet where the curve closes.

### Open curves

An open pipe has its ends closed by discs: a vertex in the middle, the
circle of the end, the normal along the tangent — outwards. The triangles
turn counterclockwise around that normal.

The curve of the second pipe goes through six points (in yellow): a
**Catmull-Rom spline**. Between the points `p1` and `p2`, the curve is a
cubic whose tangents are those of the chords `p2 − p0` and `p3 − p1`; the
ends are extended by symmetry. The spline is sampled at 32 points a
segment, and the pipe swept along the points.

## Further

- **Another profile:** the circle of the pipe is a curve in the plane
  `(r, s)`. Any closed curve there sweeps another section — a square
  section for a beam, the superellipse of the vase.
- **A varying radius:** the radius can depend on the length along the
  curve. The normal of the vertex then tilts with the slope of the radius,
  as the normals of the meridian of the vase.
- **Many pipes:** the meshes of a network of pipes, static, are merged by
  [static batching](/fdw-engine/tutorials/static-batching/).
