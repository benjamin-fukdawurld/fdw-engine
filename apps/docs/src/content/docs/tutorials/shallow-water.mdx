`@fdw/engine-fluids` simulates fluids on the GPU, in compute graphs, on
every backend. It has three methods, each for its kind of fluid:

- **a field of heights** (this tutorial): the water of a pond, a river, a
  pool, over a ground of any shape;
- **a grid of cells**: [smoke and fire](/fdw-engine/tutorials/smoke/);
- **particles**: [water that splashes](/fdw-engine/tutorials/liquid/).

The field of heights is the cheapest: one value of water per column, a few
thousand columns. It cannot make water fall over itself (a breaking wave, a
waterfall), but it fills basins, runs down slopes, overflows, and carries
ripples.

## A field of heights

```ts
import { ShallowWater } from '@fdw/engine-fluids';

const water = new ShallowWater(backend, {
  resolution: [128, 128], // cells along x and z
  cell: 0.125, // m
  origin: { x: -8, z: -8 }, // the corner of the grid
  ground: (x, z) => groundAt(x, z), // or a Float32Array, or a number
  level: 1.2, // the water at the start, where the ground is lower
  maxDepth: 1.5,
});

// Each frame:
water.update(dt);
```

Each cell is a column of water standing on its ground. Its state is in two
storage buffers of `vec4`:

- **the cells**: the depth of water, the occupancy (the bodies in the
  column, see below), the velocity of the water (x and z);
- **the fluxes**: the water leaving toward each of the four neighbors, in
  m³/s.

| Option       | Default       | What it does                                                                  |
| ------------ | ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `resolution` | —             | The cells along x and z                                                       |
| `cell`       | 0.25          | The side of a cell, in meters                                                 |
| `origin`     | centered on 0 | The corner of the grid in the world                                           |
| `ground`     | 0             | The height of the ground: per cell, a function of the position, or one height |
| `level`      | none          | Fills the cells up to this height at the start (and at `reset()`)             |
| `edges`      | `'closed'`    | `closed`: walls around the grid; `open`: the water leaves by the edges        |
| `damping`    | 0.2           | How fast the waves fade, per second                                           |
| `maxDepth`   | 2             | The deepest water expected: the steps are short enough for its waves          |
| `stiffness`  | 6             | How much of the water a body pushes aside: k / (k + 1)                        |
| `readback`   | `true`        | Reads the water back for the physics, a frame late                            |

## Pipes between the cells

The water moves through **virtual pipes** between neighboring cells
(O'Brien and Hodgins, Mei et al.). Each step is two dispatches:

1. **The fluxes.** The surface of a cell is H = ground + depth (+ the
   bodies). The flux toward a neighbor grows with the difference of their
   surfaces, dt · g · cell · (H − H<sub>neighbor</sub>), and never goes
   negative. Then all the fluxes of a cell are scaled down so that it gives
   no more water than it has: a cell can dry, never go below zero.
2. **The depths.** Each cell gains what its neighbors send it and loses
   what it sends: d += dt · (in − out) / cell². The velocity of the water
   comes from the fluxes through the cell.

Nothing is created nor lost: the water of the grid is the same from a step
to the next (a test checks it), but for the sources and the open edges.

**The steps.** The waves of shallow water run at √(g d): over 1.5 m of
water, 3.8 m/s. A step must not let them cross more than a cell:
`update(dt)` splits the frame into as many steps as `maxStep` (half the
time a wave takes to cross a cell at `maxDepth`) asks. With cells of
12.5 cm on 1.5 m of water, that is 2 steps a frame at 60 Hz.

The GPU and the CPU run the same steps (`stepShallowWater`): the rendering
tests check that they agree, on every backend.

## Sources and splashes

```ts
// A spring: 0.15 m³/s over a disc of 40 cm.
const spring = water.addSource({ x: -2.5, z: -2, radius: 0.4, rate: 0.15 });
// A drain: a negative rate.
water.addSource({ x: 3.5, z: -3.5, radius: 0.5, rate: -0.26 });
spring.remove();

// 150 liters dropped at once: a splash, ripples.
water.splash(x, z, 0.4, 0.15);
```

A source pours its rate over the cells whose centers are in its disc, the
same depth in each: the volume is exact whatever the size of the cells.

## Drawing the water

```ts
import { WaterSurface } from '@fdw/engine-fluids';
import { createSky, OceanRenderer } from '@fdw/engine-ocean';

const sky = createSky(backend);
const surface = new WaterSurface(water, { environment: sky }).setSun(light);
const oceanRenderer = new OceanRenderer(new Renderer(backend));

// Each frame, after water.update(dt):
oceanRenderer.render({
  ocean: surface,
  items: [terrain],
  camera,
  surface: canvas,
});
```

After its steps, `update` writes a third buffer for the rendering: the
height of the surface, its slopes and the speed of the water in each cell.
The `WaterSurface` is a grid over the cells, moved up to the water by the
**material of the sea** of [the ocean](/fdw-engine/tutorials/ocean/): the
same reflection of the sky, the same sun, the same ground seen through the
water and absorbed with its depth. The `OceanRenderer` draws any such
`WaterItem` over the scene drawn first.

- **The shores**: the dry cells are discarded (the material is masked), the
  edge of the water halfway between a wet center and a dry one; their
  vertices go a little under the ground, so that the water cuts the terrain
  instead of flickering on it.
- **The foam**: where the water runs fast (from `foamSpeed`, 2 m/s), the
  material sees squeezed waves, and foams. The stream of the basin below is
  white where it runs down the hill.
- **The look**: fresh water by default (`freshWaterLook`), clearer than the
  sea; `setLook` takes any uniform of `WaterLook`.

## What floats on it

<Example name="basin" />

Drag to turn around, the wheel to zoom, WASD to move; **Space** drops a
crate, **B** a ball, **E** a splash, **F** stops or starts the spring,
**R** starts again.

Bodies and water act on each other, both ways:

```ts
import { FluidBodies } from '@fdw/engine-fluids';
import { Buoyancy } from '@fdw/engine-ocean';

// The water feels the bodies: their shapes in a storage buffer.
const bodies = new FluidBodies(backend);
bodies.add(crate); // a body of @fdw/engine-physics: its box collider

// The bodies feel the water: Archimedes, and the current.
const buoyancy = new Buoyancy(water, { density: 1000 });
buoyancy.add(crate, {
  points: boxPoints(half),
  volume: 8 * half.x * half.y * half.z,
});
buoyancy.install(scheduler);

// Each frame:
bodies.update(); // where the bodies are, how they move
water.update(dt, bodies);
```

**The bodies push the water.** `FluidBodies` writes the spheres and boxes
of the bodies (their colliders, or a shape given to `add`) into a storage
buffer, six `vec4` each, that the graphs of all the fluids read. Here, each
column measures the part of each body under its water: that **occupancy**
raises the surface of the column, and the fluxes push the water away
toward the neighbors. A crate dropped in the pond makes a wave; a floating
one pushes aside the water it displaces. Not all of it: a column whose
surface would follow the body exactly has no stable state (it rings like a
U-tube); `stiffness` (6) pushes 6 / 7 of it aside, stably.

**The water carries the bodies.** `heightAt(x, z)` and `velocityAt(x, z)`
read the cells back from the GPU, one frame late. The `Buoyancy` of the
ocean takes anything with `heightAt`; with `velocityAt`, the water also
drags each wet point **toward its velocity**: a crate in the stream goes
down with it.
