Spheres from smooth to rough, red plastic in front, gold behind. A sun
lights them, but most of what they show comes from the **sky**: its
blue above, its ground below, its sun reflected sharp on the smooth
spheres and blurred on the rough ones. This is **image-based lighting**:
the light of the environment, from every direction.

Click the canvas: **E** turns the environment on and off. Without it,
the metals are black wherever they do not reflect the sun.

## An environment

```ts
const environment = new Environment({ sky: skyFunction });
renderer.render({ items, camera, surface, lights: [sun], environment });
```

The source of an `Environment` is one of:

- `{ panorama }`: an equirectangular image, a 2d texture twice as wide as
  high (the HDR panoramas): its top row is the zenith, its middle column
  looks toward −z. Linear colors, above 1 for the sun: `rgba16float`;
- `{ cube }`: a cube texture;
- `{ sky }`: a function graph of the direction, a procedural sky. Its
  input `direction` is a normalized `vec3`, its output `color` a linear
  `vec3`:

```ts
const f = new GraphBuilder('sky');
const o = new Ops(f);
const d = f.functionInput('direction', BuiltinTypes.vec3f);
const up = d.swizzle('y');
f.functionOutput(
  'color',
  o.select([0.1, 0.09, 0.08], [0.4, 0.55, 0.85], o.greater(up, 0)),
);
const skyFunction = defineFunction(f.build(), { id: 'my.sky' });
```

The options: `intensity` (1) multiplies its light, at no cost; `size`
(128) the texels of its prefiltered maps, the sharpness of the mirror
reflections; `samples` (64) the samples of the prefiltering.

## Prefiltered once

A rough surface reflects a wide cone of directions: summing them for
each pixel would cost hundreds of samples. The renderer prefilters the
environment once, on the GPU, on its first frame:

1. it **captures** the source into a panorama, with its levels;
2. it fills an **atlas**: the light reflected by surfaces of 6
   roughnesses (0, 0.2… 1), the light received by a white diffuse
   surface for each normal (the irradiance), and a table of the share of
   the light a surface reflects by its angle and roughness.

The pixels then read a few texels of the atlas. Call
`environment.update()` when the source changes (a texture rewritten, a
sky whose sun moves): it is prefiltered again on the next frame.

## In the shading models

- `pbr`: the diffuse color × the irradiance of the normal, plus the
  light of the reflected direction × the share reflected (Fresnel), both
  × `occlusion`. This is what makes metals look like metal.
- `phong`: the irradiance, and reflections as rough as its highlights
  are wide.

The `ambient` of the lights is still added: give the lights of a scene
with an environment no ambient. A custom shading model calls
`stdEnvironment.light` (black without an environment), or reads the
functions `environment.radiance(direction, roughness)`,
`environment.irradiance(normal)` and `environment.brdf(nDotV,
roughness)`.

## Showing the sky

The environment lights the scene, it is not drawn. The example draws it
on a sphere around the camera: an unlit material calling the same sky
function in the direction of the view.

```ts
const s = new GraphBuilder('sky dome');
const view = s.call(std.normalize, {
  x: s.call(std.sub, {
    a: s.call(stdMapping.worldPosition),
    b: s.engine('camera.position'),
  }),
});
s.surface({ baseColor: s.call(skyFunction, { direction: view }) });
```

For a panorama, sample it at `environment.equirect(direction)`; for any
source, `environment.radiance(direction, 0)` gives the sharpest level of
the atlas.

## Shadows and occlusion

The environment casts no shadow: a sphere on the ground gets the light of
the sky under it too. The sun's shadows ground the objects; the
`occlusion` of the surface (a texture, ambient occlusion) darkens the
light of the environment in the creases.

The [path tracer](/fdw-engine/tutorials/path-tracing/) sees the same
environment: `tracer.render({ camera, surface, environment })`, its rays
lost showing the panorama.
